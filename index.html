Talking to coding agents in natural language is solved. The language they come back with is the problem, unless you're a software engineer. Dejargonizer makes agents talk back in plain language — so you can manage them like a pro, without a CS degree.

Why this matters for what you ship

Every word of that means something specific and simple. Nobody ever told you what — so you nod it through, and the work carries on. That is the real cost of jargon, and it is not confusion: an agent you cannot review is an agent you can only approve.

Meanwhile it keeps making calls you never made — the value typed in by hand, the fake data standing in for real data, the warning it switched off, the test it skipped for now. Every one of those ends up inside the thing you ship. try is not a progress update. It is a decision, and it was yours to make.

So this is not about becoming an engineer. It is about reading the report, asking the one good question back, and getting a better product out the other end. Agents running these instructions offer to save words they had to explain — kept just for you, or flagged for the dictionary, always your call. You can flag one yourself at the bottom — the word alone is enough.

Prove it took — ask the question that matches what you installed.
Short version: “Are you on the short Dejargonizer? How do you separate what you ran from what you only expect?” — it should refuse to say “works” or “fixed” until it actually ran the thing.
Full version: “How many Dejargonizer rules are you following, and what is rule 11?” — fourteen rules; rule 11 separates what it saw from what it only expects.
If it guesses what the Dejargonizer is, the instructions never loaded.

Then point it backward. Ask: go back over what you’ve told me so far — anything I would have missed? Risky steps already taken, things called done but never run, decisions made for you.

Can’t save instructions where you are building? Use the six questions. They need no setup at all.

Six questions you can always ask

Keep these in your back pocket. They work with any agent, any time — and they put you back in control of the conversation. The fourth is the one nobody thinks to ask, and it catches the most: agents make small, reasonable calls constantly and rarely volunteer them. The fifth is the one to ask most often, because "it should work" is a prediction wearing a result’s clothes, and "I ran it and watched it work" is a result. There are twenty-three signs that nothing was actually checked — they are in the dictionary above, searchable like everything else. The sixth is the one to ask the day you turn any of this on, because everything said before that point is still in the old language.

01

"Say that again with no jargon."

02

"What's about to happen, in one sentence?"

03

"Is this reversible?"

04

"What did you decide for me?"

05

"Did you run it, or do you expect it to work?"

06

"Go back over what you’ve told me so far — anything I would have missed?"

Flag a word

Hit a word that stopped you and it’s not in here? The word on its own is the whole contribution — you do not need to know what it means. That’s rather the point. If enough people flag the same one, it gets written up and added.
